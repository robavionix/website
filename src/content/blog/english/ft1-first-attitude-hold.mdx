The last post left FT-1 with a linear and nonlinear model, both verified by two independent solvers. This post's job is to bolt the first real controller onto that linear model — a pair of attitude-hold PID loops — and see whether something as structurally simple as single-loop PID can hold the aircraft's attitude through a maneuver at all. The bar here isn't high: the point isn't great control performance, it's checking whether a plain PID loop can even keep a grip on a system this complex — a full 6-DOF plant — laying groundwork for controlling the nonlinear model later, and, more than anything else, generating the material for the real deliverable of this post: a 3D animation clear enough that anyone can tell what the aircraft is doing at a glance.

Nose up, level off, bank, level off again — chase camera, top-down ground track on the side, control surfaces glowing yellow whenever they're actually moving. That's what the video shows, but not everything the underlying MATLAB code can do: a failed control surface is set up to highlight in red, but since this simplest version of PID has no fault detection or fault-tolerant logic behind it, that part doesn't show up in this video yet.

## Starting point: a model that trims and linearizes

Yesterday's verified [`ft1.avl`](/files/ft1/ft1.avl) geometry and mass file trims to a cruise attitude in Simulink, and `linearize()` returns a 12-state linear model. Designing directly on that full model is a dead end — it carries 4 near-zero poles from the position/heading states, which don't feed back into the force/moment equations at this operating point, and `pidtune`/`isstable` flag those as instability that has nothing to do with the real pitch/roll dynamics. So it was split into two reduced 4-state loops instead: pitch on `[u w q theta]` + elevator, roll on `[v p r phi]` + aileron.

![Pitch attitude-hold PID step response: 0.5 degree command, elevator deflection history](/images/ft1-pid/ft1-pid-pitch-response.png)

![Roll attitude-hold PID step response: 10 degree command, aileron deflection history](/images/ft1-pid/ft1-pid-roll-response.png)

Gains came from `pidtune(G,'PIDF',wc,pidtuneOptions('PhaseMargin',60))`, then re-expressed as a 2-DOF PID (b=1, c=0) so the derivative acts only on the measured attitude, not on the commanded step itself — the naive 1-DOF version demanded over 2500 degrees of elevator for a 1-degree step, purely from derivative kick on the setpoint jump. The 2-DOF form has identical closed-loop poles but a sane transient.

Solving the trim equations near the operating point (`Along*x + Belong*u = 0` with `theta = cmd`) turned up something else: FT-1's tail looked severely control-power-limited — holding a 1-degree *sustained* pitch change with no throttle retrim needed roughly 38 degrees of elevator. That's obviously not possible. I designed and specified every part of this aircraft myself — its maneuverability could not genuinely be this bad.

After a long stretch of troubleshooting, and digging back through pitfalls I'd already hit once during my PhD, it came back to me: AVL's control-surface derivatives come out in radians, not degrees (see Update 2 below). Honestly, I didn't catch this until quite late in this chapter of the work, which meant every nonlinear and linear model built after it, and every PID controller built on top of them, needed substantial revision.

Every control surface in the 3D model is parsed straight out of [`ft1.avl`](/files/ft1/ft1.avl)'s real CONTROL cards — aileron at 75% chord, elevator and rudder at 60% — split at exactly the hinge fraction AVL defines, into a fixed structure and a separately-rotatable control surface. Because this visualization layer uses the same no-camber flat-plate approximation as the rest of this project's geometry rendering, the hinge kinematics come out exact: every point on a given section shares that section's Z, so rotating a surface about its hinge line needs no extra correction.

The chase camera follows yaw and pitch, but deliberately *not* roll — that's what makes a bank read as "the aircraft tilting against a level horizon" instead of "the camera itself spinning." Getting this wrong the first time made the horizon look like it was swinging, not the aircraft banking.

A simple fault model sits between the PID and the plant: the controller keeps computing as if the actuator were healthy (there's no fault detection or reconfiguration here — this is for *seeing* a fault, not testing an already-reconfigured controller), but what actually reaches the aircraft, and what anti-windup sees, is the degraded or frozen value. Three colours that can't be confused with each other: healthy (yellow, brightness scaled to actual deflection), half-effectiveness (solid orange, slow pulse), stuck (solid red, faster pulse).

## What's shipped, and what's still open

[`plots/FT1_flight_animation.mp4`](/videos/ft1/ft1-pid-flight-demo.mp4) — the one embedded above — runs the **fault-free** configuration: the three-colour fault indication is built and tested, but deliberately switched off for today's delivered version, so the aircraft stays healthy throughout. Both gains are now the clean `pidtune` output at their original bandwidths, no rescaling — roll tracks its full 10-degree command to within a fraction of a degree, pitch reaches about 88% of its 0.5-degree command at the peak and is still visibly ringing when the hold ends a few seconds later.

> **Update, 2026-07-25:** the shared linear model this post's numbers and images were built on had a real bug — a Simulink harness convention silently doubled the strength of every roll/pitch/yaw rate-damping derivative versus what the AVL geometry file actually computes. Fixing it (halving those derivatives back to their correct value) made this airframe genuinely *less* damped than the plots above show, which meant both loops needed retuning: pitch kept its bandwidth (`wc = 1.10 rad/s`) since it was already close to the phugoid-damping limit either way, but roll needed a big bandwidth increase (`wc = 0.15 → 2.00 rad/s`) to recover clean tracking — and came out *better* than before as a result. The response plots and animation frames below are the retuned, post-fix versions; the roll numbers just above are the current ones. (The bug itself was found later, by building an independent nonlinear 6DOF model from the same AVL file and comparing it against this linear one — that's a post of its own, still to come.)
>
> **Update, 2026-07-29:** a second, more fundamental bug was found after the one above: AVL's `CONTROL` derivatives (`Cmd4`, `Cld3`, and the rest) are reported per **degree**, not per radian like every other derivative AVL outputs — but the shared trim/linearize script treated them as per-radian, and so did every controller gain in this post. Net effect: every control surface was modeled as 57.3x weaker than it actually is, and every gain here was tuned 57.3x too high to compensate. Closed-loop tracking was accidentally almost right regardless (an inner loop's response is invariant under `(B·k, Kp/k)` scaling), but every *deflection angle* reported above was wrong, and so was the "control-power-limited, so this demo only uses 0.5 degrees" reasoning in "Starting point" above — that whole constraint doesn't exist once the tail's real authority is modeled correctly. With the fix and a re-tune, this demo now commands a full **5-degree** pitch step (ten times bigger than before) and the same 10-degree roll step: pitch settles to 4.947° (peak 5.672°), roll to essentially 0° after an 11.431° peak, with elevator peaking at just **4.37°** (17.5% of the ±25° limit) and aileron at **2.93°** (11.7%) — nowhere near saturated. The plots and animation below are the corrected, re-tuned versions.
