This post's job is to solve that by redesigning the PID controller itself.

Same maneuver as last time — nose up, level, bank, level — but watch how flat the hold segments are now. No visible settling wobble on either axis. That's the actual deliverable today: clean, crisp, accurate execution of the commanded maneuver.

## Why a single loop was always going to struggle with this

The question was there from the very start of the design: FT-1's pitch axis has a phugoid mode — the slow trade-off between speed and altitude every fixed-wing aircraft has in some form — sitting at about 0.58 rad/s with only 4% damping open-loop. A pure attitude loop's correction has to pass through an extra integration (elevator moves pitch *rate*; rate integrates into *angle*) before it reaches the thing actually being measured. That lag puts the correction roughly out of phase with the oscillation it's supposed to be damping, so it excites the mode about as much as it suppresses it. No amount of retuning a single gain fixes a phase problem — it's a limit of the loop topology itself, and no amount of tweaking PID parameters was ever going to change that.

The standard fix, used in real flight control systems for exactly this reason, is a **cascade**: an inner loop that feeds back body rate directly (no extra integration, so it can add real damping) commanding elevator/aileron, and an outer loop that feeds back attitude and commands the inner loop's rate setpoint.

## The structural finding that decided the whole design

Trying to design the inner loop the same way as before — `pidtune(G,'PIDF',wc,...)` — failed outright, at *every* bandwidth from 0.05 to 12 rad/s: it kept collapsing to a useless pure-integral controller. The reason turned out to be exact, not approximate: the reduced-order plant from elevator to pitch rate (and aileron to roll rate) has a **zero at the origin**. That's not a numerical artifact — it's physically necessary. A constant control-surface deflection settles to a new trim *attitude*, not a permanently nonzero *rate*; if it didn't, the aircraft would spin up forever under a fixed stick input. So the DC gain from surface to rate is exactly zero, and `pidtune`'s attempt to loop-shape a plant with zero low-frequency gain using an integral term (which needs low-frequency gain to work) is fighting the plant's own physics.

The fix was to stop asking `pidtune` a question the plant can't answer. The inner loop doesn't need to track a *sustained* rate command anyway — a rate setpoint from the outer loop is transient by construction, heading back toward zero as the attitude error closes. What it needs is damping, and damping is a root-locus problem, not a loop-shaping one: sweep a plain proportional gain, watch where the lightly-damped pole pair moves, pick a gain that's actually buying meaningful damping. That's also, not coincidentally, how real aircraft rate-damper/SAS gains are usually chosen.

The outer loop turned out simpler than expected: once you cascade the closed inner loop with the exact kinematic integrator relating rate to attitude (`theta_dot = q`, `phi_dot = p`), the plant the outer loop sees already contains a free integrator — so a plain **P** controller gives zero steady-state error for free, no `Ki` needed. Both outer loops, designed independently, converged to `Ki = 0` on their own. That's the whole controller: four proportional gains, no integrators, no derivative filters, nothing to anti-wind-up.

## The result

> **Update, 2026-07-25:** the shared linear model used here had a real bug, found later while independently verifying it against a from-scratch nonlinear 6DOF model: a Simulink harness convention was silently doubling every roll/pitch/yaw rate-damping derivative versus what AVL actually computes. Fixed at the source, which makes this airframe genuinely lighter-damped than the numbers above were computed against — roll's fast mode drops from about 37% open-loop damping to about 19%, and the phugoid gets lighter too. Both cascade gains were re-swept against the corrected plant (`Kp_inner`: pitch `-120 → -160`, roll `4 → 3`) and the numbers/plots in this post are the retuned, post-fix results — both loops actually came out a little tighter than before, not worse, which says something good about how robust this cascade architecture is to exactly how damped the underlying airframe turns out to be.
>
> **Update, 2026-07-29:** a second, more fundamental bug was found: AVL's `CONTROL` derivatives are reported per **degree**, not per radian like the rest of AVL's output, but the shared trim/linearize script — and every gain in this post — treated them as per-radian. Every control surface was modeled 57.3x weaker than it really is, compensated by gains tuned 57.3x too high. Closed-loop tracking was accidentally close to right regardless (an inner loop's response is invariant under `(B·k, Kp/k)` scaling), but every deflection number above was wrong. Re-tuned against the corrected plant and re-run at a bigger, more honest command size (5° pitch, up from 0.5°; roll unchanged at 10°): pitch settles to 4.960° (cmd 5.0°) with essentially no visible overshoot, roll to 9.995° (cmd 10.0°) — both cascades this post is actually about are just as clean as before, if not cleaner. Elevator now peaks at just **1.50°** (6.0% of the ±25° limit) and aileron at **0.53°** (2.1%), both far from saturated. The plots and animation below are the corrected versions.
