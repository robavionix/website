Every FT-1 model so far has had one lumped `aileron` input — one number, mirrored left and right. That is the AVL-native convention and it is fine for design, but it makes an entire class of failure impossible to even express: *one* aileron jamming while the other keeps working. This post is about a testbed built specifically to fix that, and about the results, which were not what I expected.

## Six actuators, not four channels

The plant is the same validated nonlinear 6-DOF model from the [previous post](/blog/ft1-nonlinear-bank-compensation/), but driven through its **physical** actuator interface rather than the aerodynamic one:
```

[ ail_L   ail_R   ele_L   ele_R   rudder   Th ]

```markdown
The mixing down to AVL's channels (`d_aileron = (ail_R − ail_L)/2`, and so on) happens *inside* the plant, which means each surface upstream of it can fail on its own. A fault-injection stage sits between the controller and the aircraft:
```

[Controller] --surf_cmd(6)--> [FAULT INJECTION] --surf_act(6)--> [Plant]

```markdown
The controller has no idea a fault has occurred. It keeps computing and commanding normally; the fault stage rewrites what actually reaches the aircraft. That is why the diagnostic view is *commanded versus actual*: the moment those two traces separate is the failure.

Three failure modes are available per surface — jammed at a fixed angle, degraded to a fraction of effectiveness, or healthy — each with its own onset time, and several can be active at once.

## A jammed aileron is almost a non-event

The headline result, and the one I had wrong going in. With the right aileron frozen at neutral and a 10° bank commanded, the aircraft holds **10.00°** — indistinguishable from healthy. Peak deflection on the surviving left aileron is 3.15° of the 25° available. Nothing saturates. It recovers to wings-level exactly.

The reason is arithmetic. The controller computes one `ail_cmd` and splits it (`ail_L = −ail_cmd`, `ail_R = +ail_cmd`), so with the right side frozen at zero the effective differential `(ail_R − ail_L)/2` is exactly **halved**. Half of this airframe's roll authority is still far more than a 10–30° bank needs: full 25° of aileron buys about 220 deg/s of roll rate.

Push it to 30° and it still holds — **30.17°**, on 3.42° of aileron. Jam *both* ailerons and the aircraft cannot roll at all (φ never leaves 0.00°) but remains perfectly stable, which is a clean demonstration that **losing control authority and losing stability are two different things**.

![States with both ailerons jammed: the roll command is simply not followed, but the aircraft is stable throughout](/images/ft1-fault-injection/states-both-jammed.png)

## A hardover of the same surface departs in six seconds

Same aileron, same aircraft, same command — but jammed at **+15°** instead of at neutral. It departs at **t = 6.4 s**.

![Right aileron hardover at 15 degrees: the states diverge within seconds](/images/ft1-fault-injection/states-hardover.png)

The obvious explanation is that 15° of stuck aileron is simply too much rolling moment to fight. That explanation is wrong, and the surfaces plot is what gives it away: **the surviving aileron never even tries.** It peaks at 2.78° while 25° of throw sits unused.

15° is well inside the actuator's range, and the fault is physically trimmable — the healthy side only needs to hold +15° for the differential to return to zero. The controller cannot get there, and the reason is structural. At steady state the roll rate `p` is zero and the outer loop's rate command is capped by `p_limit`, so the largest surface deflection this cascade can ever *hold* is
```

ail_cmd_max = Kp_ph_inner × p_limit = 0.06 × 40 deg/s = 2.40°

```markdown
— not the 25° actuator limit. Sweeping the jam angle puts the boundary exactly on that prediction:

| hardover | outcome |
|---|---|
| 1.0° | survived |
| 2.0° | survived |
| **2.4°** | **survived (marginal)** |
| 3.0° | departed at 17.3 s |
| 5.0° | departed at 9.6 s |
| 15.0° | departed at 6.4 s |

Raising the inner gain does not rescue it either: at 0.12 it still departs, and at 0.3–0.6 the aileron rails and it departs *sooner*. A pure-proportional inner rate loop structurally cannot hold a steady offset. Fixing this needs integral action in the inner loop, or explicit fault detection and reallocation — a real design change, not a gain tweak.

## Multiple simultaneous faults, and a surface that lies

Faulting several actuators at once is where the commanded-versus-actual view earns its keep. Below: the right aileron jammed at neutral **and** the right elevator degraded to 30% effectiveness, both at t = 5 s.

![Combined fault: right aileron jammed and right elevator at 30 percent effectiveness](/images/ft1-fault-injection/surfaces-combined.png)

Each mode has its own signature. `ail_R` shows the command (blue) still swinging ±3° while the actual (red) sits pinned at zero. `ele_R` shows the actual tracking the command's *shape* at exactly 30% of its amplitude — degraded, not dead. Healthy channels show the two traces exactly on top of each other. The aircraft still makes the 10° bank.

A related case worth flagging: the rudder. This controller never commands rudder at all, so a rudder hardover is a pure disturbance with no loop closed around it. The aircraft survives — but holds **30.7°** of bank against a 10° command, without ever tripping a sideslip limit. That is why the verdict here has three states rather than two: `ok`, `TRACK FAIL` (survived but never met the command), and `DEPARTED`. A two-state pass/fail would have called that one a pass.

## Results across the set

Twelve configurations — one healthy baseline and eleven faults — with a 10° bank commanded at t = 10–25 s and the fault injected at t = 5 s:
```

 #   fault                            phi held  max|beta|   max V  t_end  outcome
 1   healthy                             10.00       1.29   13.15   50.0  ok
 2   ail_R jam at neutral                10.00       1.09   13.14   50.0  ok
 3   ail_R hardover +15 deg                NaN       9.70   14.06    6.4  DEPARTED
 4   ail_R 50% effectiveness             10.00       1.20   13.15   50.0  ok
 5   ele_R jam at trim                   10.00       1.29   13.07   50.0  ok
 6   both elevators jam at trim          10.00       1.28   13.33   50.0  ok
 7   rudder hardover +10 deg             30.67      13.00   20.66   50.0  TRACK FAIL
 8   throttle stuck at trim              10.00       1.28   13.27   50.0  ok
 9   ail_L hardover -20 deg                NaN      12.85   13.55    9.0  DEPARTED
 10  both ailerons jammed                 0.00       0.00   13.00   50.0  TRACK FAIL
 11  engine out                          10.00       1.32   13.60   50.0  ok
 12  combined: ail_R jam + ele_R 30%     10.00       1.12   13.00   50.0  ok

```markdown
**Both departures are hardovers. Not one jam-at-neutral departs.** That split is the whole finding, and it is a property of the controller's structure, not of how much authority the airframe has left.

Two honest caveats on this table. Preset 4 is a weak test — halving one aileron's effectiveness barely perturbs an aircraft with this much margin at 10° bank, so it proves little. And preset 11 ("engine out") looks benign only because the demo profile asks for no climb and no speed change; an engine failure with no energy demand is close to a non-event by construction. Both would need a more demanding manoeuvre to say anything real.

## Where the model stops being trustworthy

Where a run is marked `DEPARTED`, the aircraft is at 2.5× trim speed with 20° of sideslip — well outside the envelope the AVL derivatives were computed for, which is a linear expansion about a 13 m/s trim point with **no stall model**. The right reading is: *the control design fails there*. It is not a prediction of what the real aircraft does after departure, and I would not present it as one.

What the testbed does establish is narrower and more useful: **the failure boundary for this controller is at 2.4° of jammed deflection**, it is set by `Kp_ph_inner × p_limit` rather than by actuator throw, and no amount of remaining control authority moves it.
