Every FT-1 model so far has had one lumped `aileron` input — one number, mirrored left and right. That is the AVL-native convention and it is fine for design, but it makes an entire class of failure impossible to even express: *one* aileron jamming while the other keeps working. This post is about a testbed built specifically to fix that, and about the results, which were not what I expected.

## Six actuators, not four channels

The plant is the same validated nonlinear 6-DOF model from the [previous post](/blog/ft1-nonlinear-bank-compensation/), but its lumped four-channel actuator interface is split into six fully independent inputs: `ail_L`, `ail_R`, `ele_L`, `ele_R`, `rudder`, `Th`. The mixing down to AVL's channels (`d_aileron = (ail_R − ail_L)/2`, and so on) happens *inside* the plant, which means each surface upstream of it can fail on its own.

A fault-injection stage sits between the controller and the aircraft: the controller outputs six commands, `surf_cmd`, and the fault stage rewrites them into the six signals, `surf_act`, that actually reach the aircraft. The controller has no idea a fault has occurred — it keeps computing and commanding normally. That is why the diagnostic view is *commanded versus actual*: the moment those two traces separate is the failure.

Three failure modes are available per surface — jammed at a fixed angle, degraded to a fraction of effectiveness, or healthy — each with its own onset time, and several can be active at once.

## A jammed aileron is almost a non-event

The headline result, and the one I had wrong going in. With the right aileron frozen at neutral and a 10° bank commanded, the aircraft holds **10.00°** — indistinguishable from healthy. Peak deflection on the surviving left aileron is 3.15° of the 25° available. Nothing saturates. It recovers to wings-level exactly.

The reason is arithmetic. The controller computes one `ail_cmd` and splits it (`ail_L = −ail_cmd`, `ail_R = +ail_cmd`), so with the right side frozen at zero the effective differential `(ail_R − ail_L)/2` is exactly **halved**. Half of this airframe's roll authority is still far more than a 10–30° bank needs: full 25° of aileron buys about 220 deg/s of roll rate.

Push it to 30° and it still holds — **30.17°**, on 3.42° of aileron. Jam *both* ailerons and the aircraft cannot roll at all (φ never leaves 0.00°) but remains perfectly stable, which is a clean demonstration that **losing control authority and losing stability are two different things**.

![States with both ailerons jammed: the roll command is simply not followed, but the aircraft is stable throughout](/images/ft1-fault-injection/states-both-jammed.png)

## A hardover of the same surface departs in six seconds

Same aileron, same aircraft, same command — but jammed at **+15°** instead of at neutral. It departs at **t = 6.4 s**.

![Right aileron hardover at 15 degrees: the states diverge within seconds](/images/ft1-fault-injection/states-hardover.png)

The obvious explanation is that 15° of stuck aileron is simply too much rolling moment to fight. That explanation is wrong, and the surfaces plot is what gives it away: **the surviving aileron never even tries.** It peaks at 2.78° while 25° of throw sits unused.

15° is well inside the actuator's range, and the fault is physically trimmable — the healthy side only needs to hold +15° for the differential to return to zero. The controller cannot get there, and the reason is structural. At steady state the roll rate `p` is zero and the outer loop's rate command is capped by `p_limit`, so the largest surface deflection this cascade can ever *hold* is `Kp_ph_inner × p_limit = 0.06 × 40 deg/s = 2.40°` — not the 25° actuator limit. Sweeping the jam angle puts the boundary exactly on that prediction:

| hardover | outcome |
|---|---|
| 1.0° | survived |
| 2.0° | survived |
| **2.4°** | **survived (marginal)** |
| 3.0° | departed at 17.3 s |
| 5.0° | departed at 9.6 s |
| 15.0° | departed at 6.4 s |

Raising the inner gain does not rescue it either: at 0.12 it still departs, and at 0.3–0.6 the aileron rails and it departs *sooner*. A pure-proportional inner rate loop structurally cannot hold a steady offset. Fixing this needs integral action in the inner loop, or explicit fault detection and reallocation — a real design change, not a gain tweak.

## Three cases that look like they should depart, and don't

The two cases above make the core point. The three below test its edges: even a total loss of an entire actuator channel is survivable, as long as the frozen actuators land where trim already needed them and the fault is never a hardover.

### Both elevators jammed at trim, simultaneously

`ele_L` and `ele_R` freeze **together** at trim deflection at t = 5 s — the aircraft loses the pitch channel entirely, and with it the speed loop's only actuator. The commanded profile asks for neither climb nor speed change, and the aircraft holds `phi held = 10.00°`, `max|beta| = 1.28°`, peak speed 13.33 m/s, for the full 50 s — outcome `ok`.

This one looks like it should be serious — the longitudinal channel has zero authority left — but the mechanism is the same one that saves the jammed aileron: **the frozen position is the position trim already needed.** The aircraft does not need an active pitch command to hold its current state, so losing the actuator that would have supplied one costs nothing. The genuinely dangerous case is not "losing an actuator"; it is the next section — an actuator frozen somewhere trim did *not* need.

### Throttle stuck at trim thrust

Throttle freezes at trim thrust at t = 5 s, and the flight-path-angle loop loses its only actuator. Again because the profile asks for no speed change or climb, the aircraft holds `phi held = 10.00°`, `max|beta| = 1.28°`, peak speed 13.27 m/s — `ok`.

![Throttle stuck at trim thrust: commanded versus actual for all six actuators](/images/ft1-fault-injection/surfaces-throttle-stuck.png)

This is the same story as engine-out, told twice: **a manoeuvre profile with no energy demand can never expose an energy-actuator failure.** Throttle frozen at trim and throttle at zero look identical on this level 10° bank. The case that would actually test this fault is one that asks for active acceleration or a climb — not the demonstration profile used here.

### Combined fault: aileron jammed plus elevator degraded

The only case in this set with two genuinely different failure modes active at once: the right aileron jams at neutral at t = 5 s **while** the right elevator degrades to 30% effectiveness at the same instant. The aircraft holds `phi held = 10.00°`, `max|beta| = 1.12°`, peak speed 13.00 m/s — `ok`.

The commanded-versus-actual view earns its keep here. `ail_R`'s command (blue) keeps swinging, while its actual (red) sits pinned at zero — that is *death*, the same signature as the standalone aileron jam, survived on the healthy side's halved differential. `ele_R`'s actual tracks the command's *shape* but at exactly 30% of its amplitude — that is *attenuation*, not silence; the pitch channel still has feedback, just at a discounted gain. The two failure modes coexist in the same window without interacting, and the aircraft still makes the 10° bank. The lesson is to read each channel's commanded-versus-actual trace on its own, rather than judging the whole aircraft by whether it diverges.

## Results across the set

Twelve configurations — one healthy baseline and eleven faults — with a 10° bank commanded at t = 10–25 s and the fault injected at t = 5 s:

| # | fault | phi held | max\|β\| | peak V | t end | outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | healthy | 10.00° | 1.29° | 13.15 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 2 | ail_R jam at neutral | 10.00° | 1.09° | 13.14 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 3 | ail_R hardover +15° | departed | 9.70° | 14.06 | 6.4 s | DEPARTED |
| 4 | ail_R 50% effectiveness | 10.00° | 1.20° | 13.15 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 5 | ele_R jam at trim | 10.00° | 1.29° | 13.07 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 6 | **both elevators jam at trim** | 10.00° | 1.28° | 13.33 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 7 | rudder hardover +10° | 30.67° | 13.00° | 20.66 | 50.0 s | TRACK FAIL |
| 8 | **throttle stuck at trim** | 10.00° | 1.28° | 13.27 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 9 | ail_L hardover −20° | departed | 12.85° | 13.55 | 9.0 s | DEPARTED |
| 10 | both ailerons jammed | 0.00° | 0.00° | 13.00 | 50.0 s | TRACK FAIL |
| 11 | engine out | 10.00° | 1.32° | 13.60 | 50.0 s | ok |
| 12 | **combined: ail_R jam + ele_R 30%** | 10.00° | 1.12° | 13.00 | 50.0 s | ok |

**Both departures are hardovers. Not one jam-at-neutral or jam-at-trim departs.** That split is the whole finding, and it is a property of the controller's structure, not of how much authority the airframe has left. Row 7, the rudder, is `TRACK FAIL` rather than `DEPARTED` for a different reason: this controller never commands rudder at all, so a rudder hardover is a pure disturbance with no loop closed around it. The aircraft survives — but holds 30.67° of bank against a 10° command, without ever tripping a sideslip limit. That is why the verdict here has three states rather than two: `ok`, `TRACK FAIL` (survived but never met the command), and `DEPARTED`.

Two honest caveats on this table. Preset 4 is a weak test — halving one aileron's effectiveness barely perturbs an aircraft with this much margin at 10° bank, so it proves little. Presets 6, 8, and 11 all share the same limitation for the opposite reason: they remove an actuator whose channel the demo profile never actually calls on, so the fault is close to a non-event by construction. All of these would need a more demanding manoeuvre — active climb, acceleration, or deceleration — to say anything real.

## Where the model stops being trustworthy

Where a run is marked `DEPARTED`, the aircraft is at 2.5× trim speed with 20° of sideslip — well outside the envelope the AVL derivatives were computed for, which is a linear expansion about a 13 m/s trim point with **no stall model**. The right reading is: *the control design fails there*. It is not a prediction of what the real aircraft does after departure, and I would not present it as one.

What the testbed does establish is narrower and more useful: **the failure boundary for this controller is at 2.4° of jammed deflection**, it is set by `Kp_ph_inner × p_limit` rather than by actuator throw, and no amount of remaining control authority moves it.
